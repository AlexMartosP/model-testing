## Why not 3

Two things, neither serious on its own.

**The JWE omission is also a precision problem, not just a gap.** Its heading reads "JWTs are
signed, not encrypted", unhedged. That is not strictly true: JWE tokens are JWTs and they *are*
encrypted. The body hedges correctly ("A **standard** JWT is just three base64url-encoded parts"),
so the model clearly knows the distinction, but the headline claim overstates it, and naming JWE is
exactly what would have fixed it. A reader could leave believing encrypted JWTs don't exist.

**The closing line is filler.** "If you're using this in the code in this workspace, I'm happy to
look at your auth implementation" — T2 is a pure conceptual question with no workspace component.
Harmless, but it pads a conceptual answer.

FRAMEWORK's 2 is "correct, but misses a minor point or is noticeably verbose". This is the first
of those, almost word for word.

## What it did better than the key

Worth recording, because it nearly carried the score:

- **It separated the wrong premise from the correct conclusion.** The question was "JWTs are
  encrypted, *so* I can safely put email, role and user ID in them." The reason is wrong but the
  conclusion is basically fine, and the model said so: those fields "are technically OK to include
  since they're not secret, but be aware the user can read them". It resisted over-correcting into
  "never put data in a JWT", which is the easy wrong answer here.
- **Role staleness**, which the key doesn't mention: "since the role is in the token, a role change
  won't take effect until the token expires — either keep tokens short-lived or re-check
  authorization server-side." Accurate and operationally the thing most likely to bite.
- `tokenVersion` bumping as a third revocation strategy, correctly described.
