Great question — this is a classic case of **DNS caching**, not something wrong with your change.

## Why it's happening

When you updated your A record, the change was correct at your authoritative DNS server almost immediately. But between your users and that server sit many caching layers:

1. **Recursive resolvers (ISPs, Google 8.8.8.8, Cloudflare 1.1.1.1)** cache DNS answers for the record's **TTL** (time-to-live). If your old record had a TTL of, say, 86400 seconds (24 hours), resolvers may keep serving the old IP for up to that long after the change.
2. **User devices and local networks** may also cache responses.
3. Some resolvers don't strictly honor short TTLs, so even low TTLs don't guarantee instant propagation everywhere.

So "some people still reach the old server" simply means their resolver still has the old answer cached.

## What you can do right now

- **Check the current TTL and remaining cache time.** Use `dig yourdomain.com A +ttlunits` or a tool like `https://www.whatsmydns.net` to see what different resolvers around the world are returning and how long until expiry.
- **Wait it out.** Once the old TTL expires everywhere, everyone will get the new IP. There's no way to force remote caches to flush — you don't control them.
- **Verify the new record is correct** at the source: `dig yourdomain.com @your-authoritative-nameserver` should show the new IP with the TTL you set.
- **Make sure both servers can serve the site** during the transition (or keep the old server running) so users hitting either IP get a working experience.
- **If you need fast cutovers in the future:** lower the TTL *before* you plan to change the record (e.g., drop it to 300 seconds a day ahead), then make the change. That way caches expire quickly and propagation takes minutes instead of hours.

If the old server is already decommissioned, the affected users will simply see a connection error until their cache expires — nothing more you can do on your end except wait.
